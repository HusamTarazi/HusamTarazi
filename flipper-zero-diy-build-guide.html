Expectations & the law

You're building a handheld multi-tool: Sub-GHz radio, infrared, iButton, NFC, 125 kHz RFID, GPIO / BadUSB, plus screen and D-pad. Everything except the two radios below is very achievable.

🎯

Reality check

Sub-GHz, IR, iButton, GPIO/BadUSB, display and buttons will work well. NFC (13.56 MHz) and especially 125 kHz RFID are the hard parts — the real Flipper uses a custom analog front-end that cheap modules don't fully replicate. Treat 125 kHz as a "version 2" goal and don't let it block the rest of the build.

⚖️

Legal & safety — actually read this

Only transmit to or test hardware and networks you own or have written permission for. Replaying someone's remote, cloning cards you don't own, or transmitting on licensed bands can be a crime. Radio spectrum is regulated (FCC and equivalents).

Li-ion cells are dangerous shorted or mischarged — use a protected cell and a proper charger. Soldering tips hit 350 °C: ventilate and don't breathe flux fumes.

01 · Understand before you buy

How a Flipper is wired

One brain (the MCU) talks to several modules over two shared buses. SPI is a fast bus the big peripherals share — three common wires (SCK / MOSI / MISO) plus a unique Chip-Select per device. Single-pin extras (IR, speaker, iButton) just toggle one GPIO each.

The one rule of this build

Wiring must match the exact pin map your firmware fork expects — the pins are hard-coded. You don't pick pins freely; you copy the firmware's map. That's why firmware choice comes before final wiring.

03 · The smart way

Build order

Build so every stage leaves you with a working, testable device — this is the habit that separates finished projects from drawer projects.

  1. Flash & boot the bare MCU — prove board + cable + drivers before wiring anything.
  2. Screen + buttons — now you have visual feedback for everything after.
  3. SD card, then CC1101 — the most satisfying working radio.
  4. IR, speaker, iButton — easy single-pin wins.
  5. NFC, then 125 kHz — the hard ones, last.
  6. Battery + case — once the electronics are proven on the bench.

Dead subsystem? 90% of the time it's one of these

(a) swapped MISO/MOSI · (b) wrong or shorted CS pin · (c) a 3.3 V module on 5 V (or vice-versa) · (d) a cold solder joint. Multimeter + wiring log will find it.

09 · You have a printer

The 3D-printed case

  • Search Printables / Thingiverse / GrabCAD for "DIY Flipper Zero case", "FCFZ case", or check the firmware repos' own STL/STEP files.
  • Measure your assembled stack — DIY module heights vary; verify internal dimensions before a long print.
  • PETG (tougher, pocket-friendly) or PLA (easy). ~0.2 mm layers, 3+ walls, 20–30% infill.
  • Leave openings for USB-C, SD slot, IR window, antenna, GPIO header, button pad (0.2–0.4 mm clearance).
  • No fit? Model a simple sandwich enclosure in Tinkercad (free) or FreeCAD — a great next CAD skill.

10 · Keep going

Now you're into embedded systems

  • Learn STM32 basics in STM32CubeIDE: blink an LED → read a button → talk to an I2C sensor. Makes the firmware stop feeling like magic.
  • Read the real firmware (open source, GPL): browse applications/, furi/ (its little OS) and targets/; the pin map lives in furi_hal_resources.h. Try building with fbt and writing a tiny .fap app.
  • Easy second build: flash Bruce onto a cheap ESP32 or an M5Stack Cardputer — different architecture, quick win, genuinely does more on WiFi.
  • Get a ~$10 logic analyzer — seeing SPI/I2C traffic is a debugging superpower.
