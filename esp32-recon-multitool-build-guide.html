Legal & ethical ground rules

This is a learning and authorized-testing tool. The same radios that let you study a protocol can disrupt other people's devices, and that crosses from education into a crime. Treat the rules below as the real spec of the project.

Only operate on targets you own or have written permission to test

Your own gear only. Your home WiFi, your own RFID fobs, your own remotes, a lab network you built. For anything else you need explicit written authorization (a signed scope, a CTF's rules, a lab you were invited into).

Transmitting can be illegal. WiFi deauthentication, RF/Bluetooth jamming, and flooding are denial-of-service attacks. In the US the FCC prohibits signal jammers outright, and unauthorized access to networks violates the CFAA (and equivalents worldwide). Cloning someone else's access card or intercepting private traffic is a separate offense.

Prefer receive-only learning. You can learn an enormous amount by listening — sniffing your own packets, reading your own tags, wardriving to see how 802.11 beacons work — without ever transmitting an attack. Start there.

If a feature's only purpose is to degrade or intrude on a system you don't control, don't use it. This guide documents how to build and understand the device; how you operate it is on you.

The learning surface

What each radio teaches you

Bruce turns one small board into a survey of the wireless world. Each add-on module opens a band and a set of concepts. Everything here is standard, documented functionality from the Bruce project.

WiFi 2.4GHzon-board ESP32

802.11 fundamentals: beacons, probe requests, channels, and handshakes. Scan networks, capture your own EAPOL handshake to study WPA2, stand up an "evil portal" captive page on your own lab AP, and log networks (wardriving) with a GPS module.

Bluetooth / BLEon-board ESP32

How devices advertise and pair. Scan for BLE advertisements, inspect service UUIDs, and explore how beacons and spam frames work on the 2.4GHz band.

Sub-GHz RFCC1101 · 315/433/868/915

OOK/ASK modulation on the ISM bands — the physics behind garage remotes, weather sensors, and doorbells. Read, save, and replay your own devices to understand rolling vs. fixed codes.

NFC / RFIDPN532 · 13.56 / 125kHz

How contactless tags identify themselves. Read UIDs, dump and study MIFARE Classic sectors on your own cards, and learn why some tag types are trivially cloneable and others aren't.

2.4GHz transceiverNRF24L01+

Raw packet radio outside WiFi — channel hopping, addressing, and how cheap wireless peripherals talk. A great intro to software-defined-ish RF concepts.

InfraredIR LED + TSOP receiver

Consumer-IR protocols (NEC, RC5). Capture a remote's codes and understand carrier frequency and encoding — the "universal remote" you build yourself.

Optional extras: a GPS module (ATGM336H / NEO-6M) enables map-logging of WiFi surveys; a microSD card stores captures and logs; a 125kHz reader adds low-frequency RFID study.
Pick your hardware

01Choose a build path

Two routes to the same firmware. Path A is the fastest way to a fully-featured device with barely any soldering. Path B is the "build it yourself from modules" route — cheaper per part, maximum to learn, more assembly.

Recommended · least soldering

Path A — LilyGo T-Embed CC1101

~$45–65 · 1–2 hrs

An all-in-one board that already has the screen, battery, rotary encoder, IR, and a built-in CC1101 sub-GHz radio. Bruce supports it directly. You mostly print a case and flash.

  • Screen + LiPo + encoder + IR already on board
  • Sub-GHz works out of the box (no wiring)
  • Add PN532 (NFC) & NRF24 via the Grove/QWIIC-style header
  • Excellent 3D-printed case ecosystem (see Print section)
Most modular · most to learn

Path B — ESP32-S3 + module stack

~$28–90 · 3–6 hrs

Start from a bare ESP32-S3 board (or the "Cheap Yellow Display" CYD) and wire each radio yourself. You learn the SPI/I2C bus, power, and pin mapping — and can pick exactly which capabilities to include.

  • Cheapest entry (a CYD is ~$12–18)
  • Choose your own modules & antennas
  • Real soldering + wiring practice
  • Fully custom 3D-printed enclosure
Not sure? If you want it working tonight, buy the T-Embed CC1101 (Path A). If the building is the point and you want to understand every wire, do Path B. The rest of this guide covers both; Path B has the extra wiring steps.

Standard operating procedure

03Assembly, step by step

Do it in this order. Verify each stage before moving on — it's far easier to debug one module than six at once. Path A people can skim phases 2–4 (your radios are on-board) and focus on the header add-ons and the case.

1

Bench-test the bare board first

Before any soldering, plug the core board into USB and confirm it enumerates on your computer. This proves the board and your cable are good — a shocking number of "dead boards" are just charge-only USB cables.

  • Use a USB-C data cable, not a charge-only one.
  • Confirm a COM/serial port appears (Device Manager / ls /dev/tty*).
  • Flash Bruce now (jump to the Flash section) so you have a known-good baseline before adding hardware.
2

Plan the bus & pin map

The radios share one SPI bus (CC1101, NRF24, display, SD) — each gets its own CS (chip-select) line. NFC (PN532) runs on I2C. GPS uses a UART. Write your pin map down now; you'll type it into Bruce's config later.

*Example pins only. The exact free GPIOs depend on your board (CYD, S3 devkit, T-Embed all differ). Pick pins that aren't already used by the screen/PSRAM, then set them in Bruce → Config → Pins / RF Config. There is no single "correct" map — Bruce lets you assign them.
3

Solder the SPI radios (CC1101 + NRF24)

Mount both modules on a small perfboard "hat," tie their SCK/MOSI/MISO to the shared bus, and run separate CS lines. Power CC1101 from 3.3V (never 5V). The NRF24 is noise-sensitive — solder a 10–100µF cap across its VCC/GND right at the module to stop brownouts.

  • Keep RF wires short; twist power pairs.
  • Attach the 433MHz and 2.4GHz antennas before powering up — transmitting without an antenna can damage the RF stage.
  • Test: boot Bruce, open the sub-GHz menu, and confirm the CC1101 is detected (no "module not found").
4

Wire NFC (I2C) and IR

Set the PN532's onboard DIP switches to I2C mode, then connect SDA/SCL/3V3/GND. For IR, drive the IR LED through an NPN transistor (the GPIO can't source enough current alone) and wire the TSOP receiver's data pin to your IR-RX GPIO.

  • I2C needs pull-ups — most PN532 boards include them.
  • Verify NFC by reading your own transit/hotel card's UID.
  • Verify IR by capturing a button from your TV remote.
5

Power: battery, charging & switch

If your board doesn't have charging built in (the T-Embed does), wire the LiPo through a TP4056 protected charger and put an SPDT slide switch on the battery's positive line so the device fully powers down. This solves the "battery flat in 2 days" complaint on some boards.

  • Observe polarity — reversed LiPo will destroy the board.
  • Insulate the cell with Kapton tape; never let solder joints touch the pouch.
  • Charge the first time on a fireproof surface and watch it.
6

Dry-fit, then close the case

Test-fit everything in the printed shell before final assembly. Set your heat-set inserts with the soldering iron (low temp, press straight down), route antennas to their SMA holes, seat the magnets, and screw it together.

  • Add foam/Kapton so nothing shorts against the battery.
  • Leave the USB-C port and SD slot accessible.
  • Do a final full-feature boot test before you seal it.
Firmware

04Flash the Bruce firmware

Bruce installs straight from the browser — no toolchain to set up. Use desktop Chrome or Edge (they support Web Serial); Firefox and Safari won't work for the flasher.

  • 1 · Open the web flasher. Go to bruce.computer/flasher (the official installer). Keep the GitHub repo and wiki open for reference.
  • 2 · Connect over USB-C. Plug in the board with a data cable. Click Connect and pick the serial port. If nothing shows, install the CP210x / CH340 USB-serial driver for your board.
  • 3 · Enter bootloader if needed. Most boards auto-enter. If flashing fails, hold BOOT, tap RST/EN, then release BOOT to force download mode.
  • 4 · Select your exact device. Choose your model (T-Embed CC1101, CYD-2432S028, generic ESP32-S3). Picking the right target sets the correct display & pin defaults.
  • 5 · Install & wait. Click Install/Flash. It erases and writes in 1–3 minutes. Don't unplug mid-flash.
  • 6 · Reboot & configure. On first boot, open Config and set your pin map and RF Config (rfTx/rfRx, CS lines) to match your wiring from Phase 2. Set the RTC/clock and brightness while you're there.
  • 7 · Verify each module. Walk the menus: WiFi scan, BLE scan, sub-GHz (CC1101 detected), NFC read your own card, IR capture a remote. Green across the board = you're done.
Updating later: re-run the web flasher any time to get new Bruce releases, or download the .bin from GitHub Releases and flash with esptool. Your config is stored on-device.
3D printing

05Print the enclosure

You've got a printer — use it. There's a mature ecosystem of case STLs, especially for the T-Embed CC1101. Print the shell, add heat-set inserts, and you've got a rugged handheld.

Where to get STLs

  • Printables — search "T-Embed CC1101". Excellent shells with external-antenna holes, power-switch mods, and GPS-module room.
  • Thingiverse / Yeggi — search "ESP32 CYD case" or "Bruce ESP32" for the CYD and devkit routes.
  • Design your own — the boards are simple rectangles; a parametric case in Tinkercad/Fusion/OpenSCAD is very doable.

Recommended print settings

  • Material: PETG (heat + drop resistant) or PLA+ for easy printing.
  • Layer height: 0.2mm · Walls: 3 perimeters · Infill: 20–30%.
  • Supports: only on port cutouts / overhangs; use tree supports.
  • Flexible bits (buttons, antenna grommets): TPU 95A.
  • Inserts: heat-set M2/M3 with the soldering iron at ~200°C.
Pro tip: print the case after you've dry-fit your electronics on the bench, so you can confirm the STL matches your exact board revision and module stack before committing filament.
